NZS 3404 and the NZGS guidelines for site investigation require a solid understanding of subsurface stratigraphy before any significant foundation design or earthworks can proceed in Gisborne. The city sits on the edge of the actively deforming Hikurangi subduction margin, where the complexity of the Plio-Pleistocene sedimentary sequences and the underlying Cretaceous greywacke basement demands more than just point-source borehole data. Seismic tomography provides a continuous 2D or 3D velocity model of the ground that directly informs seismic site class (NZS 1170.5) and reveals hidden paleochannels or variable rockhead depths. When we acquire data along a traverse in the Whataupoko hills or near the Waipaoa River floodplain, the resulting P-wave and S-wave velocity sections give engineers the spatial resolution they need to interpolate between CPT soundings with confidence and to avoid surprises during bulk excavation. This is not a supplementary tool in Gisborne—it is often the only practical method to map the lateral continuity of the young, soft sediments that define the Poverty Bay flats.

A seismic velocity model reveals more about the ground in one transect than a dozen boreholes placed on a grid without geophysical context.

Technical details of the service in Gisborne

The coastal environment around Gisborne, with its high seasonal rainfall, deeply weathered mudstone profiles, and the ever-present influence of the subduction interface just offshore, creates specific challenges for seismic wave propagation that our acquisition parameters must address. Surface waves and cultural noise from the port and State Highway 35 can mask the weak refracted arrivals from deep interfaces, so we routinely deploy high-sensitivity 4.5 Hz geophones and use a 24-bit seismograph with pre-stack filtering to extract clean first breaks. The refraction method works particularly well here for mapping the top of the competent Tolaga Group sandstone beneath the overlying alluvium, while high-resolution reflection profiling—often processed with advanced velocity analysis—can resolve subtle features like buried slip surfaces within the landslide-prone hill country. For projects where the shear-wave velocity profile is the primary deliverable, we complement the tomography with an active MASW survey along the same spread, which provides a direct Vs30 measurement for site classification without the uncertainty of correlating from SPT N-values alone. In the deeper basins south of the city, we have also integrated these results with resistivity imaging to discriminate between saline groundwater intrusion and fresh-water-bearing gravels, a distinction critical for long-term foundation durability.

Local geotechnical conditions in Gisborne

Gisborne's position above the Hikurangi subduction zone means that deep, soft sedimentary basins can amplify seismic shaking in ways that a simple one-dimensional soil profile would miss. The Poverty Bay flats are underlain by up to 200 m of Late Quaternary alluvial and marine sediments with shear-wave velocities often below 200 m/s in the upper 30 m, pushing many sites into Site Class D or E. A misinterpretation of the depth to the engineering bedrock—especially where a thin, high-velocity gravel layer masks a deeper low-velocity zone—can lead to an unconservative seismic design. The 2007 Gisborne earthquake (M6.8) demonstrated how local site effects generated variable damage patterns across the city, closely correlated with basin geometry. Seismic tomography directly images the velocity structure that governs this response, giving the structural engineer a defensible model for site-specific response analysis. Skipping this step and relying solely on sparse borehole logs in a basin-edge setting is a recognized source of geotechnical risk that the NZGS guidelines explicitly caution against.

Our services

Our seismic tomography services in Gisborne are structured to support the full project lifecycle, from feasibility to detailed design. Each survey is planned by a geophysicist and reviewed by a chartered geotechnical engineer to ensure the results are directly applicable to the foundation design problem.

Refraction Tomography for Bedrock Mapping

Aimed at determining depth to competent rock for heavy foundations, bridge piers, and landslide stabilization works. We use multi-shot, multi-receiver arrays and first-arrival traveltime tomography to produce a continuous compressional-wave velocity model. The final deliverable includes interpreted engineering layers (topsoil, weathered zone, competent rock) overlaid on the velocity section, correlated with any available borehole control and classified per NZGS terminology. This is the standard method for greenfield site investigations in the Wharerata and Makaraka areas where basalt flows and mudstone interbeds create sharp lateral contrasts.

High-Resolution Seismic Reflection for Basin Imaging

Deployed for deep investigations requiring stratigraphic detail beyond the penetration limit of refraction. A closely spaced geophone spread and a high-frequency source (buffalo gun or mini-vibrator) are used to acquire CDP-gathered data, which is processed through a full sequence including deconvolution, NMO correction, and pre-stack time migration. This service is particularly effective for imaging the contact between the Quaternary basin fill and the Tertiary bedrock beneath the Waipaoa floodplain, a critical input for any liquefaction assessment that requires accurate basin geometry.

FAQ

What is the difference between seismic refraction and reflection, and which does my Gisborne project need?

Refraction tomography uses the first arrival of seismic energy that has travelled along a velocity boundary to map a layered earth model. It works best when velocity increases with depth—typical when soft alluvium overlies mudstone or greywacke. Reflection profiling records the seismic energy that bounces back from acoustic impedance contrasts and can resolve deeper, more complex geometries, including velocity inversions. For a shallow foundation on the flats where you need top-of-rock within 30 m, refraction is faster and more cost-effective. For a deep basin study or where you suspect a low-velocity layer beneath a gravel cap, we recommend reflection.

How does seismic tomography help with NZS 1170.5 site classification in Gisborne?

Site classification requires either a measured shear-wave velocity profile (Vs30) or a correlation from SPT N-values. Seismic refraction gives us a P-wave velocity model; when combined with a co-located MASW survey, we can derive the full 2D shear-wave velocity section. This allows us to calculate Vs30 along the entire transect, not just at a single borehole location. In variable ground like the Poverty Bay margin, this spatial coverage often reveals that a site straddles two different soil classes, which has direct implications for the design spectra.

What are the typical costs for a seismic tomography survey in the Gisborne region?

For a standard refraction tomography line of 115 to 230 m with 24 to 48 channels, a single-day mobilization to a site in the Gisborne area typically falls between NZ$4,410 and NZ$9,430 plus GST. The range depends on the line length, the number of shot points required to achieve the target depth, and the terrain difficulty—access across steep, scrub-covered hillsides takes longer and may require extra crew. A combined refraction and reflection survey with deeper penetration will sit at the upper end of that bracket and may require a second processing day. Every proposal includes a specific scope based on the site conditions we observe during the initial walkover.

Can you perform seismic surveys on steep, vegetated slopes in the Gisborne hill country?

Yes, we routinely work on slopes exceeding 25 degrees across the Whataupoko and Wainui hinterlands. The key is using a portable weight-drop source that does not require a vehicle, and laying out the geophone string along a cleared foot track. We apply topographic corrections during the tomographic inversion using a detailed digital terrain model of the line, so the final velocity section is referenced to true ground surface. The processing workflow accounts for the statics introduced by the slope, which is particularly important when the refraction line is being used to interpret the depth to a basal slip surface for a slope stability analysis.

How do you validate the seismic velocity model against actual ground conditions?

We calibrate every tomography line against intrusive data wherever it is available. In Gisborne, we often recommend a single targeted SPT borehole or a series of test pits at key stations along the seismic traverse to provide a direct lithological tie to the velocity boundaries. The P-wave velocity of a saturated East Coast alluvial gravel, for instance, is well constrained in our regional database, but local cementation or a high fines content can shift that value. The borehole log gives us the ground truth to refine the velocity-to-geology conversion and ensures the final interpretive cross-section is not just geophysically valid but geotechnically defensible.
