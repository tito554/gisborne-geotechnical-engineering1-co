NZS 3404 and the NZGS guidelines frame every raft foundation we design in Gisborne, but local experience is what makes them buildable. The Poverty Bay flats and river terraces between the Waipaoa and Taruheru catchments are underlain by Holocene alluvium—loose sands, soft silts, and pockets of estuarine clay that struggle under concentrated loads. A conventional isolated footing often triggers differential settlement here, which is why we default to a rigid mat when the bearing stratum is deeper than two metres or the site sits within 500 m of the coast. Groundwater is typically high, salinity is present in the eastern suburbs, and the city’s seismic profile (Peak Ground Acceleration around 0.45g per the NSHM) demands a foundation that ties the structure together rather than letting it drift apart. Before we commit to a mat, we usually run a few test pits to confirm the depth to natural gravels and check for buried organic layers that compromise stiffness.

A well-designed raft in Gisborne does not just support the building—it forces the whole footprint to move as one unit during an earthquake, protecting the superstructure from differential drift.

Technical details of the service in Gisborne

A recent project on a sloping section off Wainui Road showed exactly why Gisborne keeps us honest. The client had cut into a paleo-channel filled with uncompacted silt and thin lenses of pumice sand—material that looked firm in the bucket but softened dramatically under repetitive loading. We modelled the raft as a cellular slab with edge beams deepened to 900 mm, bearing on a compacted gravel raft 300 mm thick, all sitting above a geotextile separator. The structural stiffness of the mat bridged the weaker pockets while the gravel layer acted as a drainage blanket to keep excess pore pressures from building during a seismic event. Across the wider Tairāwhiti region, we also encounter cemented Pleistocene gravels within two metres of the surface; in those cases, a ground-supported slab with uniform thickness works well, but we still specify a perimeter thickening to control edge rotation. When the soil profile varies sharply—and it does here—we often supplement the investigation with a CPT test to get a continuous log of tip resistance and friction ratio, which feeds directly into our subgrade reaction modulus for the FEM model.

Local geotechnical conditions in Gisborne

Gisborne’s combination of coastal humidity, warm summers, and occasional heavy rainfall from sub-tropical lows creates a moisture cycle that punishes poorly drained foundations. The natural soils east of the airport and around Kaiti have a high silt fraction that retains water, and when you add the cyclic pore pressure generated during a moderate earthquake—think the 2007 Gisborne event—you get a temporary loss of bearing stiffness precisely when the structure needs it most. We design the raft sub-base as a free-draining layer connected to perimeter subsoil drains, and we always check for liquefaction susceptibility in the upper 5 metres using CPT-based methods. In zones where the fine sand layers are loose enough to trigger, we combine the raft with Improvement such as stone columns to densify the critical horizon and provide drainage paths, reducing the risk of post-liquefaction settlement to within tolerable limits.

Our services

Our raft and mat foundation scope in Gisborne covers the full engineering chain—from subsurface characterisation through to reinforcement detailing and construction-phase QA. Every design is backed by a laboratory accredited to ISO 17025 for soils testing, ensuring the stiffness and strength parameters fed into the structural model are defensible under peer review.

Geotechnical investigation for raft design

Boreholes, CPT soundings, and test pits across the Poverty Bay flats to define the bearing stratum, groundwater regime, and liquefaction potential. We log to NZGS guidelines and deliver a factual report with interpreted design parameters.

Structural design and FEM modelling

3D finite-element analysis of the soil–raft interaction using Plaxis or SAFE, including seismic load cases per NZS 1170.5. Deliverables include reinforcement schedules, edge beam details, and pile–raft transition zones where required.

Construction support and QA

Subgrade proof rolling inspections, compaction testing of the gravel raft, concrete pour supervision, and anchor bolt verification. We work alongside the Tairāwhiti building consent authority to close out conditions efficiently.

FAQ

At what depth does a raft become more economical than deep piling in Gisborne?

When competent ground (N-value > 15 in sands or undrained shear strength > 50 kPa in clays) is within 3.0–3.5 m of the underside of the slab, a raft with edge beams typically costs less than a piled solution. The exact crossover depends on column loads, but we run a comparative estimate early in the concept phase so the project team can make an informed call.

What is the typical cost range for a raft foundation design package in Gisborne?

A full design package—geotechnical investigation, laboratory testing, FEM analysis, and PS1/PS4 documentation—generally falls between NZ$1,590 and NZ$6,440 depending on the building footprint, number of boreholes, and seismic complexity. We provide a fixed-price proposal after reviewing the architectural plans and site access.

How do you handle the high groundwater table common in the Wainui–Makaraka area?

We specify a 300 mm minimum clean gravel drainage blanket beneath the slab, coupled with a geotextile separator to prevent fines migration. Perimeter subsoil drains discharge to a sump or stormwater system, and we often add a waterproof membrane below the slab for habitable spaces. During construction, we manage dewatering with well-points if the excavation extends more than 500 mm below the standing water level.

Does a raft foundation eliminate the need for liquefaction assessment?

No. A raft improves post-liquefaction performance by distributing loads and reducing differential settlement, but it does not prevent liquefaction in the underlying soil. We still run a full screening using CPT data and, where the factor of safety is below 1.2, recommend ground treatment such as stone columns or rammed aggregate piers beneath the mat footprint.
